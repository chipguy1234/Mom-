# NYSTCE ESOL CST (116) Study Simulator — Content Spec

This spec is for the people (agents) writing the question bank and lessons. Read all of it before writing.

## 1. The real exam (research summary)

- **Test:** NYSTCE Content Specialty Test, Field 116: *English to Speakers of Other Languages* (ESOL). It is computer-based and delivered by Pearson.
- **Format:** 90 selected-response (multiple-choice) items with 4 options (A–D) and one best answer, plus **1 extended constructed-response item**.
- **Timing:** 195 minutes total. About 135 minutes is planned for the selected-response items (roughly 90 seconds per item) and up to 60 minutes for the constructed response.
- **Scoring:** Selected-response items are 80% of the score and the constructed response is 20%. Scaled scores run 400–600, and **520 passes**.
- **Competencies (official names and weights):**

| Code | Competency | Weight | Approx. SR items |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0001 | Language and Language Learning | 13% | 15 |
| 0002 | Knowledge of English Language Learners | 13% | 15 |
| 0003 | ESOL Instructional Planning, Practices, and Assessment | 13% | 15 |
| 0004 | Instructing English Language Learners in English Language Arts | 14% | 15 |
| 0005 | Instructing English Language Learners in the Content Areas | 14% | 15 |
| 0006 | ESOL Professional Environments | 13% | 15 |
| 0007 | Analysis, Synthesis, and Application (the constructed response) | 20% | 1 CR |

- **Constructed response (0007):** a scenario-based task that requires analyzing several artifacts, such as student background, NYSESLAT results, teacher observation notes, and oral or writing samples. The official sample is a fifth-grade Integrated ENL/ELA class early in the school year with three ELLs (for example, a 10-year-old from Puerto Rico whose home language is Spanish and who had bilingual instruction through grade 4). Candidates analyze the students and write about 400–600 words. Scoring uses a 4-point scale on three performance characteristics:
  - **Completeness:** the degree to which the response addresses all parts of the assignment.
  - **Accuracy:** the degree to which the response demonstrates relevant knowledge and skills accurately and effectively.
  - **Depth of Support:** the degree to which the response provides appropriate examples and details that demonstrate sound reasoning.
  - The score points are 4 = thorough command, 3 = general command, 2 = partial command, and 1 = little or no command.
- **Item style (NES/Pearson):** stems test *pedagogical content knowledge* far more than recall.
  - Typical stems:
    - "An ENL teacher in a grade 7 Integrated ENL science class notices… Which of the following strategies would be **most effective**…?"
    - "This student's error is most likely the result of…"
    - "The teacher's approach best reflects which of the following principles of second-language acquisition?"
    - "Which of the following best describes…"
  - Many items include a short **stimulus**, such as a student writing sample, an oral transcript, a lesson-plan excerpt, a data table, a parent note, or a word list.
  - Distractors are *plausible*. They are often partly true, too narrow, deficit-oriented, decontextualized, or right for a different proficiency level.

## 2. NYS terminology to use

Use these terms throughout:

- ELL (you may also say ELL/MLL or multilingual learner; NYSED now says "English Language Learners/Multilingual Learners").
- ENL (English as a New Language), with **Stand-alone ENL** and **Integrated ENL**.
- Bilingual Education programs: Transitional Bilingual Education (TBE) and Dual Language (one-way and two-way).
- NYSITELL (identification test) and NYSESLAT (the annual spring test of listening, speaking, reading, and writing).
- The five proficiency levels: **Entering, Emerging, Transitioning, Expanding, Commanding**.
- Home Language Questionnaire (HLQ), CR Part 154, and the Blueprint for ELL/MLL Success.
- New Language Arts Progressions (NLAP) and Home Language Arts Progressions (HLAP).
- Next Generation ELA Learning Standards, and the Culturally Responsive-Sustaining Education (CR-SE) Framework.
- SIFE, former ELLs, Long-Term ELLs, and ELLs with disabilities.

## 3. Canonical facts (keep every item consistent with these; do NOT invent other precise numbers)

**NYS identification and placement (CR Part 154)**

- At enrollment the district gives the **Home Language Questionnaire (HLQ)**, with an individual interview in English and the home language by qualified personnel.
- If a language other than English is indicated, the student takes the **NYSITELL**.
- Identification must be complete within **10 school days** of initial enrollment.
- Parents get notification of ELL status within **5 school days** of the determination, in their preferred language.
- Parents attend an orientation about program options (TBE, Dual Language, ENL).
- A **re-identification** review can be requested by a parent/guardian, teacher (with parent consent), or the student (only if 18 or older) within 45 school days of enrollment.
- **Language Proficiency Team (LPT):** for a student with an IEP, the LPT decides whether the student takes the NYSITELL. Its recommendation goes to the superintendent.
- **SIFE** are ELLs who have attended school in the U.S. for less than 12 months and who, on initial enrollment, are two or more years below grade level in home-language literacy and/or math because of interrupted or inadequate schooling. SIFE status is determined within 30 school days, using an oral interview questionnaire and, for certain languages, the MLS (Multilingual Literacy SIFE Screener).
- **ELL subgroups:**
  - Newcomers: 0–3 years of ELL services.
  - Developing ELLs: 4–6 years.
  - Long-Term ELLs: completed 6 or more years.
  - Former ELLs: exited, and receive 2 years of continued support and testing accommodations.
  - ELLs with disabilities.
  - SIFE.
- **Bilingual program trigger:** a district must provide a bilingual education program when there are **20 or more ELLs with the same home language in the same grade level districtwide**. Parents may opt for ENL.
- **NYSESLAT exit:**
  - Score **Commanding**, OR
  - Score **Expanding** AND score Level 3 or higher on the Grades 3–8 ELA test, or 65 or higher on the Regents ELA exam.
- **ENL units of study, grades K–8 (minutes per week):**
  - Entering: 360 (180 Stand-alone + 180 Integrated ENL/ELA).
  - Emerging: 360 (90 Stand-alone + 180 Integrated ENL/ELA + 90 flexible).
  - Transitioning: 180 (90 Integrated ENL/ELA + 90 flexible: Stand-alone or Integrated).
  - Expanding: 180 (Integrated ENL in ELA or another core content area).
  - Commanding (former ELL): 90 (Integrated ENL or other approved former-ELL services) for 2 years.
  - Grades 9–12: Entering is 540 minutes per week and the other levels follow the K–8 pattern. Don't write items that hinge on the 9–12 breakdown.
- **Testing accommodations for ELLs and former ELLs** (former ELLs keep them for 2 years after exit):
  - Extended time (for example, time and a half).
  - Separate location or small group.
  - **Bilingual glossaries** (word-to-word, no definitions) on content tests.
  - Translated editions of math, science, and social studies tests, or oral translation for lower-incidence languages.
  - Writing responses to content-test items in the home language.
  - **None of the translation or glossary supports apply to ELA tests.**
- **First-year exemption:** ELLs in their first year in U.S. schools may be exempted once from the Grades 3–8 ELA test (they take the NYSESLAT instead).
- **Professional development (CTLE):** teachers need 175 hours every 5 years.
  - At least **15%** of the hours must address language acquisition and ELL needs.
  - For certified ESOL and bilingual teachers, at least **50%**.
- **NYS documents:**
  - **Blueprint for ELL/MLL Success:** 8 principles. Every teacher is a teacher of ELLs. Leaders are responsible. Instruction is rigorous and grade-level. Leverage ESOL and bilingual expertise. Value and build on cultural and linguistic assets. Leverage home language and culture. Use diverse and valid assessments. Partner meaningfully with families.
  - **CR-SE Framework (2019):** four principles: a welcoming and affirming environment; high expectations and rigorous instruction; inclusive curriculum and assessment; ongoing professional learning.
  - The **Parents' Bill of Rights for NYS ELLs**.
  - The **NYS Seal of Biliteracy**.
  - The **Aspira Consent Decree (1974, NYC)**, which established bilingual instruction for Spanish-speaking students in NYC.
- **NLAP/HLAP** (Bilingual Common Core Progressions): for each standard they show what ELLs can do at each of the five levels, in receptive and productive modalities. The scaffolding decreases from Entering (substantial) to Commanding (independent).

**Federal laws and cases**

- Title VI, Civil Rights Act of 1964: no discrimination by race, color, or national origin in federally funded programs.
- Bilingual Education Act, 1968 (Title VII of ESEA).
- May 25, 1970 OCR Memo: districts must take affirmative steps to open instruction to language-minority students. They must not assign students to special education because of English skills, must not exclude them from college-prep tracks, and must notify parents in a language they understand.
- **Lau v. Nichols (1974):** a Supreme Court case brought by Chinese-speaking students in San Francisco. It held that the same facilities, textbooks, teachers, and curriculum do not equal equal opportunity, under Title VI.
- EEOA (1974): requires "appropriate action to overcome language barriers."
- Lau Remedies (1975).
- Serna v. Portales (1974).
- **Castañeda v. Pickard (1981, 5th Circuit):** a three-prong test. Programs must be (1) based on sound educational theory, (2) implemented effectively with adequate resources and personnel, and (3) evaluated and shown effective after a trial period.
- **Plyler v. Doe (1982):** states may not deny free public K–12 education to undocumented children (14th Amendment, Equal Protection).
- Meyer v. Nebraska (1923): struck down a ban on teaching foreign languages to young children.
- Keyes v. School District No. 1, Denver (1973).
- NCLB (2001), Title III.
- **ESSA (2015):** moved English-proficiency accountability into Title I. It requires standardized statewide entry and exit procedures and reporting on long-term ELs and ELs with disabilities. Former ELs may stay in the EL subgroup for up to 4 years for accountability.
- 2015 DOJ/OCR Dear Colleague Letter: EL obligations covering identification, programs, qualified staff, avoiding unnecessary segregation, special-education evaluation, opt-outs, monitoring, program evaluation, and parent communication.
- California Prop 227 (1998), Arizona Prop 203 (2000), and Massachusetts Question 2 (2002) were English-only initiatives (CA Prop 227 was repealed by Prop 58 in 2016).
- IDEA: evaluations must be in the child's native language and not be the result of limited English proficiency.

**Theory**

- **Krashen:** the Acquisition–Learning, Monitor, Natural Order, Input (i+1), and Affective Filter hypotheses. Natural Approach stages (Krashen & Terrell): Preproduction/silent, Early Production, Speech Emergence, Intermediate Fluency, Advanced Fluency.
- **Cummins:** BICS (about 1–3 years) and CALP (about 5–7 years, longer without home-language schooling). Also the Common Underlying Proficiency (iceberg), the Threshold and Developmental Interdependence hypotheses, and the context-embedded/context-reduced × cognitively undemanding/demanding quadrants.
- **Interaction and output:** Swain's Output hypothesis (noticing the gap, hypothesis testing, metalinguistic reflection). Long's Interaction hypothesis (negotiation of meaning through comprehension checks, clarification requests, and confirmation checks). Schmidt's Noticing hypothesis.
- **Sociocultural and innatist:** Vygotsky's ZPD and sociocultural theory, and scaffolding (Wood, Bruner & Ross). Chomsky's LAD and Universal Grammar. Lenneberg's Critical Period. Skinner's behaviorism, which underlies the Audiolingual Method.
- **Errors and interlanguage:** Selinker's interlanguage and fossilization. Lado's Contrastive Analysis. Corder's error analysis (errors vs. mistakes).
- **Competence and motivation:** Hymes coined "communicative competence," and Canale & Swain defined its parts (grammatical, sociolinguistic, discourse, strategic). Gardner & Lambert: integrative vs. instrumental motivation. Schumann's Acculturation Model (social and psychological distance). Berry's acculturation strategies (integration, assimilation, separation, marginalization). Typical stages of cultural adjustment: honeymoon/euphoria → culture shock/hostility → recovery/humor → adjustment/adaptation.
- **Program research:** Thomas & Collier on program effectiveness and the time to academic parity.
- **Culture and translanguaging:** García on translanguaging (CUNY-NYSIEB). Moll's funds of knowledge. Ladson-Billings' culturally relevant pedagogy. Paris's culturally sustaining pedagogy. Gay's culturally responsive teaching.
- **Instruction:**
  - Beck, McKeown & Kucan: vocabulary Tiers 1, 2, and 3.
  - Chamot & O'Malley: CALLA (metacognitive, cognitive, and social/affective strategies).
  - Echevarría, Vogt & Short: SIOP, with 8 components (Lesson Preparation, Building Background, Comprehensible Input, Strategies, Interaction, Practice & Application, Lesson Delivery, Review & Assessment) and 30 features.
  - Walqui: QTEL and scaffolding.
  - Lyster & Ranta: corrective feedback types (explicit correction, recast, clarification request, metalinguistic feedback, elicitation, repetition). Recasts are the most common type, while elicitation and metalinguistic feedback produce more learner-generated repair.
  - Methods: Asher's TPR; Gattegno's Silent Way; Lozanov's Suggestopedia; Curran's Community Language Learning; Grammar-Translation; the Direct Method; Audiolingual; CLT; Task-Based Language Teaching; Content-Based Instruction; the Language Experience Approach.
  - Co-teaching models: one teach/one observe, one teach/one assist, station, parallel, alternative, and team teaching.

If an item depends on a fact NOT in this list, use it only if you are certain it is accurate and uncontroversial. Otherwise test the concept, not the detail.

## 4. Skill map (every question gets exactly one skill code)

**Competency 1 — Language and Language Learning**
- 1.1 Phonology & pronunciation: phonemes, minimal pairs, allophones, stress, intonation, syllable structure, and L1 phonological transfer (e.g., Spanish epenthesis "espeak," a missing /ʃ/–/tʃ/ contrast, Japanese/Korean /r/–/l/, Arabic /p/–/b/, consonant clusters, final consonants).
- 1.2 Morphology & word formation: free/bound, inflectional vs. derivational, affixes, compounding, blending, clipping, acronyms, back-formation, and morphological analysis.
- 1.3 Syntax & grammar: word order, clauses and sentence types, tense/aspect, articles, count/noncount nouns, phrasal verbs, passive voice, and analyzing ELL grammatical errors.
- 1.4 Semantics, pragmatics & discourse: polysemy, idioms, connotation, speech acts, implicature, register, turn-taking, cohesion/coherence, discourse markers, and communicative competence (Canale & Swain).
- 1.5 Theories of second-language acquisition: Krashen, Cummins (BICS/CALP, CUP, thresholds), interaction/output/noticing, Vygotsky, Chomsky/UG, behaviorism, and the critical period.
- 1.6 L2 development processes: stages, the silent period, interlanguage, fossilization, overgeneralization, positive/negative transfer, code-switching, developmental sequences, errors vs. mistakes, learner strategies (cognitive, metacognitive, social-affective), metalinguistic awareness, and the NYS proficiency levels as developmental descriptors.
- 1.7 Sociolinguistics, language & culture: dialects (e.g., African American English as rule-governed), register and style shifting, World Englishes, language and identity, language attitudes and status, and heritage languages.

**Competency 2 — Knowledge of English Language Learners**
- 2.1 NYS identification & ELL subgroups: the HLQ, interview, NYSITELL, timelines, parent notification, re-identification, the LPT, and subgroup definitions.
- 2.2 Individual learner factors: age, aptitude, motivation types, personality, anxiety and the affective filter, prior schooling, L1 literacy, and learning styles.
- 2.3 Culture, acculturation & identity: stages of cultural adjustment, Berry, Schumann, cross-cultural communication norms (eye contact, wait time, collectivist/individualist, high/low context), and bicultural identity.
- 2.4 Culturally responsive-sustaining, asset-based teaching: funds of knowledge, the CR-SE framework, avoiding stereotypes, and home language and culture as resources.
- 2.5 SIFE, newcomers, refugees & trauma-informed practice.
- 2.6 ELLs with disabilities: distinguishing language difference from disability, pre-referral and MTSS/RTI with ELLs, evaluation in the home language, IEPs that address language needs, and avoiding over- and under-identification.
- 2.7 Long-Term ELLs, gifted ELLs & other profiles: LTELLs (oral fluency vs. academic literacy), gifted and talented identification, heritage speakers, dialect speakers, and former ELLs.

**Competency 3 — ESOL Instructional Planning, Practices, and Assessment**
- 3.1 NYS program models & Part 154 service requirements: Stand-alone and Integrated ENL, TBE, Dual Language, units of study, the bilingual program trigger, and parent choice.
- 3.2 Approaches & methods: recognizing and evaluating CLT, CBI, SIOP, TPR, the Natural Approach, Audiolingual, Grammar-Translation, the Direct Method, the Silent Way, Suggestopedia, CLL, TBLT, the LEA, CALLA, and translanguaging pedagogy.
- 3.3 Planning: content and language objectives, language functions and forms, linguistic demands, scaffolding by proficiency level, gradual release, and backward design.
- 3.4 Comprehensible input, interaction & feedback: teacher talk, wait time, visuals and realia, cooperative structures, structured academic talk, and corrective feedback types.
- 3.5 NYS language-proficiency assessment: the NYSITELL and NYSESLAT (modalities, levels, exit criteria, using NYSESLAT data).
- 3.6 Classroom assessment principles: formative vs. summative, validity, reliability, bias, authentic and performance assessment, portfolios, rubrics, self- and peer assessment, and norm- vs. criterion-referenced tests.
- 3.7 Testing accommodations & data-driven differentiation.

**Competency 4 — Instructing ELLs in English Language Arts**
- 4.1 Next Gen ELA standards & the New Language Arts Progressions: using the NLAP to set level-appropriate targets and scaffolds for grade-level standards.
- 4.2 Oral language development: academic conversations, sentence frames, accountable talk, listening comprehension, and pronunciation in context.
- 4.3 Foundational literacy & L1–L2 literacy transfer: phonological awareness, phonics, decoding, fluency, alphabetic vs. non-alphabetic L1, cross-linguistic transfer, and literacy for preliterate students.
- 4.4 Vocabulary development: tiers, cognates and false cognates, morphology, explicit instruction, multiple-meaning words, idioms, and collocations.
- 4.5 Reading comprehension & complex texts: building background, scaffolding instead of simplifying, pre/during/post reading, close reading, reciprocal teaching, think-alouds, and graphic organizers.
- 4.6 Writing development: process writing, genres, interactive and shared writing, dialogue journals, mentor texts, sentence combining, writing feedback, analyzing ELL writing samples, and L1 rhetorical patterns.
- 4.7 Literature, culture & home language in ELA: multicultural and culturally relevant texts, translanguaging in ELA, bilingual texts, identity texts, cultural schema, and figurative language.

**Competency 5 — Instructing ELLs in the Content Areas**
- 5.1 Sheltered instruction & content–language integration: SIOP, CALLA, CBI, and content and language objectives in content classes.
- 5.2 Integrated ENL co-teaching & collaboration with content teachers: co-teaching models, co-planning, and roles.
- 5.3 Mathematics: the language of word problems, multiple-meaning words (table, product, volume, odd), symbols, manipulatives, and cultural differences in notation and algorithms.
- 5.4 Science: inquiry and hands-on work, lab language, passive voice and nominalization, hypothesis/prediction frames, science notebooks, and visuals.
- 5.5 Social studies: chronology, cause/effect, primary sources, background knowledge, maps and timelines, and multiple perspectives.
- 5.6 Academic language across disciplines: language functions (describe, compare, explain, argue), signal words, text structures, complex sentences, nominalization, and general academic vocabulary.
- 5.7 Assessing content learning of ELLs: separating content from language, reducing the language load, visuals, performance tasks, home-language responses, glossaries, and rubrics.

**Competency 6 — ESOL Professional Environments**
- 6.1 Federal laws & landmark court cases.
- 6.2 NYS policy & frameworks: Part 154, the Blueprint, CR-SE, the Parents' Bill of Rights, the CTLE requirements, the Seal of Biliteracy, and translation and interpretation rights.
- 6.3 Family engagement & communication: home language, qualified interpreters (never children), orientations and program choice, culturally responsive partnerships, and differing cultural norms about school involvement.
- 6.4 Collaboration, community partnerships & serving as a resource for colleagues.
- 6.5 Advocacy & equity: equitable access to advanced coursework, gifted programs, extracurriculars, and counseling; appropriate placement; and challenging bias.
- 6.6 Reflective practice, ethics & professional growth: reflection, action research, PD, professional organizations (TESOL International Association, NYS TESOL, NABE), and using feedback and data.

**Competency 7 — Constructed response (separate file)**
- 7.1 Identifying significant strengths from evidence.
- 7.2 Identifying significant needs from evidence.
- 7.3 Selecting an evidence-based strategy that builds on the strength to address the need.
- 7.4 Explaining effectiveness: rationale tied to evidence and theory.

## 5. Trap taxonomy (tag EVERY wrong option with exactly one code)

These codes power the "pinpoint" analytics. They tell the student *why* she keeps choosing wrong answers.

| code | meaning |
|---|---|
| TERM | Confuses two similar terms or concepts (near-miss definition). Also fill in `pair`, e.g. "BICS ↔ CALP". |
| PARTIAL | True or good, but not the BEST answer. Too narrow, incomplete, or a secondary concern. |
| DEFICIT | Treats the home language or culture as a problem; English-only; deficit view. |
| LOWER_RIGOR | Waters down content or lowers expectations instead of scaffolding grade-level work. |
| ISOLATED | Decontextualized drills or rote practice in isolation instead of meaningful context. |
| PASSIVE | Teacher-centered; students only listen or copy; no interaction or output. |
| MISREFERRAL | Confuses typical L2-acquisition behavior with a disability (or delays a warranted evaluation). |
| ONE_SIZE | Ignores the student's proficiency level or profile; the same support for everyone. |
| WRONG_LEVEL | A strategy or expectation that doesn't match the proficiency level or developmental stage. |
| OVERCORRECT | Excessive or interrupting error correction, or form over meaning when meaning is the goal. Raises anxiety. |
| EXCLUSION | Removes the ELL from grade-level, mainstream, or enrichment opportunity ("wait until proficient"). |
| ASSESS_INVALID | The assessment measures the wrong construct (language instead of content, or the reverse), or is biased or unreliable. |
| LAW_FACT | Wrong federal law or court-case detail (wrong case, holding, or year). |
| NYS_POLICY | Wrong NYS procedure, timeline, test, or requirement. |
| STEREOTYPE | Overgeneralizes or assumes things about a cultural or language group. |
| FAMILY_BARRIER | Ineffective or inequitable family communication (a child as interpreter, English-only, assuming disinterest). |
| MISAPPLIED_THEORY | Right idea, wrong theorist; or misreads what a theory implies for teaching. |
| LITERAL | Latches onto a surface detail of the scenario instead of the key evidence. Misreads what the stem asks. |

## 6. Question JSON schema

```json
{
  "id": "1.3-07",
  "skill": "1.3",
  "type": "scenario",
  "difficulty": 2,
  "diag": 0,
  "stimulus": "optional. Student writing sample / transcript / table / lesson excerpt. Plain text, \\n for new lines. Tables: lines with ' | ' separators, first line = header.",
  "stem": "Which of the following ...?",
  "options": ["A text", "B text", "C text", "D text"],
  "answer": 2,
  "why": "2–4 sentences: why the keyed answer is BEST. Teach the concept (name the term/theory/rule) so a learner who got it wrong learns from it.",
  "traps": [
    {"code": "TERM", "pair": "inflectional ↔ derivational", "note": "1–2 sentences: why this option is wrong and what misconception it reflects."},
    {"code": "PARTIAL", "note": "..."},
    null,
    {"code": "ISOLATED", "note": "..."}
  ],
  "key": "One-sentence takeaway rule for a flashcard (≤ 25 words)."
}
```

- `type` is one of: `knowledge` (direct recall of a concept or fact), `application` (apply a concept to an example), `scenario` (a classroom situation where you choose the best action), or `analysis` (interpret student work or data).
- `answer` is the 0-based index of the correct option.
- `traps` is aligned with `options`, and the entry for the correct option is `null`.

Rules:

- **4 options**, exactly one best answer, never "all/none of the above," and never "both A and B."
- Use negative stems ("NOT," "LEAST") at most once per 25 items, in caps.
- **Balance the answer positions:** across your file each index 0–3 should be the key 20–30% of the time.
- **No length cue:** the keyed option is the longest option in at most 35% of your items. Keep options parallel in grammar and length.
- Absolute words ("always," "never," "only") must not be a giveaway. Don't put them only in the wrong options.
- **Mix:** at least 55% `scenario`/`application`/`analysis`. At least 3 items per skill have a `stimulus`.
  - Difficulty: about 30% level 1, 45% level 2, 25% level 3.
  - Level 3 = two or three options look defensible and the best one requires precise knowledge.
- **diag:** in each skill, mark exactly ONE item `"diag": 1` and exactly ONE different item `"diag": 2`. Both must be representative, difficulty-2, and cover different subtopics of the skill. All other items get `"diag": 0`.
- **Cover the whole skill:** spread items across all the subtopics listed for the skill, with no two items testing the identical fact the identical way.
- **Use realistic NY classroom details:** grade levels, Integrated/Stand-alone ENL, NYSESLAT levels, and real home languages (Spanish, Chinese/Mandarin/Cantonese, Arabic, Bengali, Haitian Creole, Russian, Urdu, Uzbek, Karen, Wolof, Fulani, Nepali, Tibetan, Korean, French, Polish, Ukrainian, Pashto, Dari, Q'anjob'al, K'iche', Mixteco). People are diverse; avoid stereotypes.
- **Pedagogy items:** the best answer typically reflects these principles:
  - High expectations with scaffolds (not simplification).
  - Interaction and output.
  - Using the home language and culture as assets.
  - Evidence-based, level-appropriate choices.
  - Meaning before form.
  - Collaboration.
  - Valid assessment.
  - Equity and advocacy.

  Don't make it obvious. Distractors should also sound professional.
- **Self-check every item:** could a well-prepared ESOL expert argue that a different option is equally correct? If yes, rewrite it. Is every fact accurate per Section 3? Is the key unambiguous?

## 7. Lesson JSON schema (one per skill you own)

```json
{
  "skill": "1.3",
  "title": "Syntax & Grammar for ESOL Teachers",
  "why": "One sentence: how this skill shows up on the 116 exam.",
  "body": [
    {"h": "Section heading", "md": "Text. Paragraphs separated by a blank line (\\n\\n). Bullet lines start with '- '. **bold** allowed. Include concrete examples (student utterances, sentences, classroom moves)."}
  ],
  "traps": ["Exam trap: …", "…"],
  "terms": [{"t": "term", "d": "definition ≤ 25 words"}],
  "tip": "One-line exam strategy for this skill."
}
```

- `body` has 4–7 sections and about 700–1100 words in total. Write it like a sharp tutor: clear, warm, and specific. Use comparison bullet lists where useful (e.g., inflectional vs. derivational).
- `traps` has 3–5 entries.
- `terms` has 10–16 entries.

The audience is an experienced teacher (the user's mom) studying for this certification. Plain English, no fluff.

## 8. How to produce your file

Write a Python source file (e.g. `esol116/content/src_c1a.py`) that defines `QUESTIONS = [...]` and `LESSONS = [...]` as Python literals. Triple-quoted strings are fine for long text. End it with:

```python
import json, pathlib
pathlib.Path(__file__).with_name("c1a.json").write_text(json.dumps({"questions": QUESTIONS, "lessons": LESSONS}, ensure_ascii=False, indent=1))
```

Run it with `python3`, then run the validator and fix everything it reports:

```
python3 /home/user/Mom-/esol116/validate.py /home/user/Mom-/esol116/content/c1a.json
```

Do not touch any other files. Do not run git.
